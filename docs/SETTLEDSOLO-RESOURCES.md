# SettledSolo resources

Last reviewed: 22 September 2026

These notes are SettledSolo's own plain-language guidance for planning calm, gradual
alone-time practice. They are grounded in the evidence listed below and in cautious
practical principles: start below meaningful distress, observe what the dog actually does,
adapt to the individual and avoid repeatedly rehearsing panic. They are not a diagnosis,
clinical dosage or substitute for veterinary or qualified behaviour support.

## One-session cheatsheet

1. Choose a duration your dog has already handled comfortably. Do not use the session to
   discover a maximum by waiting for distress.
2. Keep the departure and return ordinary. Use a camera or another observation method when
   it is practical, but do not turn monitoring into a source of pressure.
3. If meaningful concern appears, return early. A target is a ceiling, never a quota.
4. Let your dog settle before deciding whether to repeat, simplify or stop for the day.
5. Record the first useful observation: duration, behaviour, environment, what happened
   between repetitions and how recovery looked.

## If a session goes badly

- End the absence and help your dog return to a calm state.
- Make the next planned step shorter and simpler; do not compensate by pushing harder.
- Check context such as illness, noise, confinement, visitors or a changed routine.
- Where possible, arrange safe coverage between planned sessions so panic is not repeatedly
  rehearsed.
- Pause timed departures and seek help for self-injury, destructive escape behaviour,
  rapidly escalating distress or repeated sessions that cannot stay manageable.

## What to record

Useful records include:

- planned and actual time away;
- the first observable change, not only barking or the final outcome;
- pacing, panting, exit-watching, vocalisation, inability to settle or destructive/escape
  behaviour when present;
- whether food or treats were offered and accepted;
- context such as crated/confined or free-roam, time of day and what happened beforehand;
- how quickly the dog returned to their normal behaviour.

The app's signals are prompts for observation, not a diagnostic checklist. A camera is
optional and should be used in a way that remains comfortable for both dog and owner.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many sessions should I do?

SettledSolo uses a conservative daily ceiling rather than a required quota. One easy
session or a rest day can be the right choice. The ceiling is a product safety boundary,
not a clinical prescription.

### What if I miss a day?

Nothing needs catching up. Resume with an easy, familiar step instead of increasing
difficulty to compensate.

### Is a crate always the right place to practise?

No. If distress appears only with confinement, treat that as important information rather
than assuming it is purely separation-related. Use a setup the dog can already manage safely
and ask for support if you are unsure.

### What if my dog looks worried before I leave?

Use departure-cue practice while staying home: handle keys, shoes, a bag or the door in a
small way, then return to ordinary activity before concern builds. Keep cue practice brief
and separate from timed absences.

### Is separation-related distress always obvious?

No. Pacing, panting, exit-watching, food refusal, freezing, repeated vocalising or
following a person closely before departure can all be useful observations. They are not
diagnostic in isolation, so consider the context and ask for help when the picture is
unclear.

### Is comfort after a return harmful?

There is no need to withhold calm comfort. Keep returns ordinary and focus on avoiding
repeated absences that push the dog into panic.

### Should medical decisions be part of the training algorithm?

No. Questions about medication, illness, confinement or procedures such as spaying or
neutering belong with a veterinarian who can assess the individual dog. The app does not
make those decisions.

### When should I stop and ask for help?

Pause timed practice for self-injury, destructive escape attempts, rapidly escalating
distress or repeated difficulty that is not becoming more manageable. A veterinarian or
qualified behaviour professional can help work out the safest next step.

## Evidence boundary

The strongest product principles are gradual systematic desensitisation and direct
observation. The studies below support those principles but do not establish a universal
progression formula, daily number of departures or exact step size. SettledSolo's target
increments, warm-up order, daily ceiling and referral thresholds are product heuristics.
